# Example — Invalidating a Core Assumption Through Discovery

---

## Assumption Tested

Users perceive **charging optimization configuration** as a primary source of friction and would adopt additional automation or configuration options to improve their charging experience.

The implicit belief was that:
- more configurability
- more optimization options
would translate into higher perceived value and adoption.

---

## Why This Assumption Mattered

The initiative was already positioned as a value driver and aligned with broader goals around smart charging differentiation.

If the assumption was wrong:
- adoption of new configuration features would be low
- added complexity would not improve the user experience
- product effort would be spent optimizing the wrong layer of the problem

In a system with high technical and operational complexity, building the wrong abstraction would have long-term cost.

---

## Discovery Signals Collected

Discovery focused on understanding **what users actually care about during charging**, not what they say they want to configure.

Signals collected included:
- qualitative interviews focused on recent charging experiences
- discussion of failed or unexpected charging outcomes
- analysis of support cases related to charging behavior
- mapping the end-to-end charging journey from plug-in to departure

The focus was on outcomes, not feature feedback.

---

## What We Found

Discovery consistently showed that:
- users did not perceive configuration or optimization settings as the core problem
- minor friction in setup existed, but was tolerated
- the dominant pain point was **unreliable charging outcomes**
- users cared far more about:
  - reaching the target State of Charge
  - predictability at departure time
  - avoiding surprises during charging

Several users explicitly stated that:
> they would not engage with additional configuration if the system could simply “get charging right” reliably.

---

## Decision

The original direction — adding more optimization or configuration features — was invalidated.

The initiative pivoted from:
- improving configuration and optimization controls  
to:
- improving **outcome reliability, monitoring, and recovery**

Discovery reframed the product goal from:
> “give users more control over charging”  
to:
> “ensure the charging outcome is consistently correct, even when conditions change”

Discovery was treated as a success, even though it invalidated an already accepted direction.

---

## Key Learning

In complex, automated systems:
- users optimize for **outcomes**, not control
- reliability matters more than configurability
- adding features can hide, rather than solve, core problems

Discovery must be allowed to **invalidate the problem framing itself**, not just refine the solution.
