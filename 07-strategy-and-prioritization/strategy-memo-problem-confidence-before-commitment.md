# Strategy Memo - Problem Confidence Before Commitment

## Evidence Metadata

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Used in | [Discovery Pivot](../01-case-studies/case-study-discovery-pivot/case-study.md) |
| Decision supported | Shift roadmap investment only when the underlying problem is validated, not when the solution is already socially accepted. |
| Evidence type | Strategy, Qualitative, Proxy |
| Confidence level | High |
| Outcome influenced | Better roadmap quality, stronger discovery discipline, and reduced low-impact delivery. |

## Decision

Roadmap confidence should not outrun problem confidence. Before protecting an already committed solution, validate whether the underlying problem is important enough to change user behavior or business outcomes.

## Context

Organizations often become confident in an initiative because the signal has been repeated often enough:

- feature requests keep appearing
- stakeholders keep naming the same friction
- the roadmap already contains a placeholder

That kind of repetition creates organizational confidence, but it does not create product confidence.

## Why This Matters

When a team commits to the solution before validating the problem:

- discovery becomes politically harder to use honestly
- teams optimize scope around the wrong premise
- roadmap delivery starts competing with product truth

Senior PM work requires knowing when to preserve certainty and when to challenge it.

## Strategic Implication

The portfolio examples in this repo support a simple strategic rule:

- commit early to the outcome you want
- stay flexible longer on the problem framing and solution shape

This is especially important when:

- the user pain is inferred more than observed
- the roadmap already assumes an answer
- the organization rewards visible progress more than corrected direction

## What Good Looks Like

A stronger strategy process asks three questions before a roadmap item hardens:

1. What user or business behavior are we expecting to change?
2. What assumption would invalidate that expectation?
3. What evidence would justify either continued investment or a pivot?

## Practical Outcome

In practice, this improves more than discovery quality. It improves portfolio quality by:

- protecting capacity from low-adoption work
- making trade-offs more explicit earlier
- giving stakeholders a clearer reason for why direction changed

Strategy is not only choosing what to do. It is also choosing what evidence must exist before commitment becomes expensive.
